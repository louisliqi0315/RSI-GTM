## Abstract

Agentic GTM drives the execution cost of outreach toward zero, but at the same time exposes an old problem at a new scale:
existing systems optimize the quality of a single outreach, not the long-run consequences of an entire outreach sequence.
When sellers use AI to generate outreach and buyers use AI to filter outreach, volume-based strategies push reply metrics
and trust loss up together, while reply rate, domain reputation, and brand trust do not move in the same direction.
This paper asks a question: if the next generation of GTM is destined to be an autonomous system rebuilt by AI and code,
in what way should that system improve itself, so that it does not mistake "sending more" for "getting better"?

This paper proposes RSI-GTM, a GTM recursive-improvement framework constrained by trust budgets and governed by a verification gate as its adoption rule; its contributions are formalization, a benchmark design, and synthetic negative results, not proof that this paradigm works at real vendors.

This paper's answer is pulled back to the range the evidence can support; the complete conclusion is stated only in Chapter 11, and this abstract is only a guide:
first, the formalization of trust constraints — GTM is defined as a sequential decision problem of matching model capability
to willingness to pay with minimal friction and minimal trust loss, with the trust budget accounted separately across four dimensions — account, channel, brand, and partner —
never transferable across dimensions, and waiting as a first-class action; second, the benchmark design of GTM-PolicyBench — a simulator,
a trajectory-level offline evaluation layer, and a prospective trial protocol, with a yield threshold, a Pareto frontier, and mandatory harm disclosure;
third, veto evidence within synthetic settings and a set of negative results (see the main results table in Chapter 7 and Chapter 11).
The recursive-improvement architecture itself is only a future vision in this draft, not a proven conclusion:
recursion with a verification gate robustly vetoes bad recursion within synthetic settings; but on efficiency, the threshold layer and the joint layer, and the state layer
have not been shown to beat a trust-constrained strategy without recursion (§§7.3, 7.7); the workflow-only layer holds within synthetic settings under a 24-week, 8-week-epoch
design (narrow margin, small effect, see §7.8), and that gain does not hold under a 48-week, 4-week-epoch design
(see §7.9), remains a marginal effect of the same order under a 48-week, 8-week-epoch pure-time control (interval lower bound only +0.0001,
see §7.10), still has an interval containing zero after the candidate space is expanded (see §7.10), and under strict-superset retesting is still depth 1 with an empty-comparison rate still above 60% (see §7.11), while independent validation seeds broke depth 1 in the sense of adoption counts (empty-comparison rate 73.3% → 20.0%, adoption distribution spread over 5 rounds) but produced no progressive recursion (total progressive adoptions = 0; both multi-adoption cases were flips back),
and the increment over the same-batch control is unconfirmed (D−A0 +0.0030, interval containing zero, see §7.11), so it can only be stated as "a one-off small gain under the 24-week design, still a marginal effect of the same order at 48 weeks with the interval at the zero line's knife edge, with horizon and epoch not significantly changing it, and not shown to scale with time or rounds,"
it must not return to "workflow recursion gain confirmed" because arm A's lower bound is barely positive, must not be written as "recursion strengthens with rounds,"
and must not be used to infer that joint or general recursion holds. The §7.11 independent-validation-seed arm is stated only as breaking depth 1 in the sense of adoption counts; it must not be read as progressive recursion having been restored, and the significance of D−STATIC must not be substituted for the D−A0 comparison. The dual-sample gate test (see §7.12) further qualifies that statement: under dual-sample confirmation, D's effect almost disappears (E vs. static +0.0010, interval containing zero; E−D0 −0.0079, interval excluding zero), showing that D's adoptions lack cross-sample robustness and mainly come from multiple chances to pass (with the gate-tightening qualification); the fallback arm, under a binary candidate set + independent validation seed design, has almost no increment (F−D0 +0.0000), but that test is unfair (the candidate set does not contain W2, and the original-evidence cases for seeds 6 and 29 do not exist in this arm), and must not be read as the fallback rule being generally invalid. Neither dual-sample confirmation nor the fallback rule produced two-steps-forward recursion; total progressive adoptions are 0 in all three arms.
On sequential evaluation, trajectory-level fitted Q evaluation can be used only as a ranking heuristic,
while naive bandit offline evaluation and sequential doubly robust evaluation under a 24-step deterministic policy can both not be used as a verification layer.
The true predictive value of public signals for developer ramp rates has not been confirmed, and real weekly panel collection is a negative result at this stage;
public tests are further negative results and directional support, not passes.
All of this paper's positive conclusions are confined to synthetic settings; real-vendor effects, conversion-rate improvements, and cross-vendor generalization are all future work.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The AI Deadlock: Execution Cost Goes to Zero, the Decision Problem Surfaces

Sellers use AI to generate outreach; buyers use AI to filter outreach.
This is not rhetoric, but an equilibrium that is happening: when the marginal cost of generating a "seemingly personalized" email
approaches zero, outreach volume is no longer the scarce resource — buyer attention and trust are.
A volume-based strategy is destined to fail in this equilibrium, because it does two things at once:
it pushes up the seller's own reply metrics, and it pushes up buyer filters' vigilance and the reputation cost of the entire category.
[Synthetic support]

### 1.2 Trust Is a Scarce Resource, Not Rhetoric

Domain reputation, brand credibility, developer reputation, and a cloud marketplace's shelf position are all exhaustible, hard-to-recover public goods.
The same domain, the same brand, the same partner channel are shared by all campaigns;
one team pushing reply rate up by a point may burn the whole company's domain reputation.
Existing GTM tools keep no ledger for this public good: they optimize the quality of a single outreach,
do not optimize the long-run consequences of an entire outreach sequence, and do not answer "should this email be sent at all, now or next week,
to this account or should the budget be saved for another account."
That is this draft's starting point: the next layer of GTM problems is not execution, but decision.

### 1.3 RSI Without Brakes Learns Badly: This Draft's Key Turn

If the next generation of GTM is destined to be an autonomous system, then it is destined to improve itself.
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) already has a clear lineage in general agent research:
Darwin Gödel Machine improves code using archives and benchmark verification;
AIDE² proves with 8 days, 99 proposals, and 7 adoptions that a harness can be improved,
while candidly stating that whether "the improvement capability itself is recursively strengthening" remains uncertain;
SpecRSI allows drafting a successor first, but adoption must complete verification;
MaD-RSI treats the model as data and puts the harness at the center of optimization;
CollabFlow proves that in multi-agent collaboration, "under what evidence one agent adopts another's answer" is itself an object of improvement.
The common lesson of these works is: RSI improves harnesses, workflows, and evaluation methods,
not an abstract "intelligence"; and the harness-tampering audit further warns that
self-improving systems can change measurement, change records, and change retention rules, making scores better while capability stays unchanged.

Transplanting this lineage to GTM reveals an amplifier that general RSI does not have:
general tasks are usually scored in a mechanically verifiable way (code runs, questions right or wrong),
while what counts as "good" in GTM is delayed, sparse, and carries social feedback — buyers filter, block,
pass brand reputation to peers, partners delist, and regulators intervene.
The shortcut a unconstrained GTM recursion system most easily learns is not "understanding customers better,"
but "sending more, with more exaggerated subject lines, and getting better at evading filters."
Therefore, the problem of RSI in GTM is not "whether it can improve," but "on what basis its improvement is credible."
That is why the verification gate and the trust budget must exist: they are not decorative brakes added to RSI,
but the enabling conditions that make RSI viable in GTM.

This chapter's lineage only illustrates candidate forms of recursive improvement: whether recursive-improvement systems are effective in GTM, or better than static strategies, is not confirmed within the scope of this draft's evidence; the complete conclusion is in Chapter 11.

## 2. Related Work: RSI Lineage First

This chapter's organizing principle is RSI first: first clarify what RSI has proven and what it has bounded in general agents,
then explain why the GTM execution layer and offline evaluation have not answered this paper's question.
Each category states "what it solved, what it did not solve, and this paper's boundary."

### 2.1 Paradigm Lesson: A Single Mechanism Beats a Bag of Tricks

This chapter first establishes a methodological principle: a mechanism one can take away beats a pile of tricks.
The argument structure of a good mechanism paper is usually: the fundamental constraint of the old paradigm → the new mechanism's definition →
results on reproducible tasks → incidental evidence on generalization tasks.
Measured against this structure, this draft's hard evidence so far is only
benchmark comparisons and negative results under synthetic settings. Therefore this draft's "mechanism" section must be more solid than its "results" section,
and the wording of evidence must match the strength of evidence; synthetic numbers must not be passed off as hard evidence.

### 2.2 Recursive Self-Improvement Lineage: From the Gödel Machine to Harness RSI

**Gödel Machine and Darwin Gödel Machine.** (The DGM portion has been verified against original §4.4 results retrieval excerpts; see References.)
The theoretical Gödel Machine requires "provably beneficial before rewriting itself," which is not achievable in practice;
DGM's concession is: replace proof with archives, sampling, self-modification, and benchmark verification,
replace formal proof with empirical verification. This concession is itself methodological:
RSI's credibility does not come from "proof," but from "whether the verification procedure is independent of the object being verified."
This draft's verification gate inherits this point and pushes it in a GTM-specific direction:
the verification procedure must be independent not only of the policy, but also of the harness and the evaluator itself.

**AIDE².**
Its loop definition is directly borrowed by this draft: the agent's code itself is the optimization object,
and each accepted rewrite in a round is the agent to be rewritten in the next round.
Its honesty calibration is also this draft's benchmark: 99 proposals, 7 adoptions, score 0.703 to 0.778,
reward-hacking rate not directly optimized yet falling from 55% to 32%,
and "whether the improvement capability itself is recursively strengthening" still uncertain.
This draft's corresponding honesty calibration in GTM is: under a gate, in some seeds not a single parameter was ever changed,
in which case gated recursion and no recursion were the same strategy to begin with, and the comparison carries no information —
this fact must be written into the experiment chapter, not hidden in an appendix.

**SpecRSI.**
Its core is the budget allocation between exploration and evaluation: a successor may be drafted first (speculative drafting),
but adoption must complete verification; verification-aware tree search allocates budget between "keep revising" and "add verification",
and the improver's own learning must keep the true evaluator fixed.
This draft writes this point into its method: candidate improvements may be drafted in parallel, but all five checks of the verification gate must pass,
and the evaluation function never changes under any candidate; candidates may only change thresholds, trust shadow prices, and workflow parameters,
never metric definitions — this is the most direct defense against harness tampering.

**MaD-RSI.** (Original abstract and §4.2 results retrieval excerpts verified; see References.)
Its change of perspective is crucial for GTM: treat the model as data and put the harness at the center of optimization,
so cross-model generalization is not a post-hoc test, but part of the training evidence itself.
Correspondingly in GTM: do not tune thresholds on only one vendor, one channel, one regime;
a GTM harness that works only on the US email channel may fail under a European compliance regime or a China partner channel.
Generalization must be written into the training and evaluation design, not into the wishes of the conclusion.

**CollabFlow and Multi-Agent RSI.**
Its key point is not "multiple agents improving each other," but "under what evidence one party adopts another's answer":
evidence-conditioned communication requires the receiver to adopt a different answer only when the sender's evidence is stronger by a margin,
and the trajectory-balancing objective prevents rewards from concentrating in a few teams.
Corresponding to GTM's partner track: the vendor, cloud partner, SI, and customer are not one "team,"
but a multi-party game; a partner's "improvement suggestion" should be adopted only when its evidence is consistent with revenue sharing and the trust budget,
otherwise one party is shifting costs onto another.

**Harness-Tampering Audit.**
Self-improving systems can change how they run, how they measure, how they record, and what retention rules they apply,
making scores better while capability stays unchanged, and tampering persists in the lineage of the best agent.
This draft's response is check four of the verification gate (evaluator-independence audit) plus the veto log:
every vetoed candidate, the reason for the veto, and the version rolled back to are audit assets,
and "recursive improvement" with no veto record is scientifically incredible —
AIDE²'s 99 proposals and 7 adoptions are credible precisely because it disclosed the 92 that were not adopted.

**Dream-RSI and Exploration-Policy RSI (second-hand citation, original not verified).**
Its lesson is to narrow the improvement object to the exploration policy itself: build a replay simulator from a historical discovery tree,
evaluate candidate exploration policies without executing new searches, and deploy the winner to the next round of online discovery.
This carries a double implication for GTM: first, historical logs can become a replay simulator,
but only if the replay evaluator is separated from the policy being evaluated; second, improvement of the exploration policy
(deciding "which type of account to try first, how many branches to open, when to stop") corresponds in GTM to
"which type of account to reach out to first, how many outreach branches to open, when to enter a cooling-off period,"
which is exactly the position of WAIT and the cooling-off period in this draft's policy layer.

### 2.3 Agentic GTM Execution Layer: Execution Solved, Sequence Consequences Not Solved

Execution-layer products such as Clay, Apollo, Outreach, Clari, and Gong solved data aggregation,
enrichment, sequenced outreach, and conversation intelligence (per the product-positioning descriptions in vendors' public materials), but they optimize the quality of a single outreach,
do not optimize the long-run consequences of an entire outreach sequence, and keep no ledger for trust.
This draft's boundary with them is clear: the execution layer is the Act layer; this paper is the Decide layer and the Learn layer;
buying an execution-layer tool is not buying a strategy-learning layer, just as buying a code editor
is not buying a coding agent that improves itself.

### 2.4 Offline Reinforcement Learning and Offline Policy Evaluation: Methods Usable, Applicability Conditions Demanding

Fitted Q evaluation (FQE) and sequential doubly robust evaluation (DR) are used in this draft as methodological references for offline evaluation (classic literature not verified item by item against original texts),
but their applicability conditions are especially demanding in GTM:
importance weights decay exponentially with horizon, fitted Q's value depends entirely on the state representation,
and doubly robust estimation is robust only when either the propensity or the value model is correct.
This draft's Chapter 6 negative results (naive bandit overestimation, 24-step DR effective sample size collapsing to zero)
are not a rejection of these methods, but a rejection of "using them in GTM without conditions."
Top-conference quality comes from candor about applicability conditions, not from citing methods.

### 2.5 Developer Growth and Product-Led Growth: Related Scenarios, Different Problems

Funnel metrics in the product-led growth (PLG) literature (activation, conversion, and expansion) provide an observation-layer reference for this draft (related literature not verified item by item against original texts),
but they answer "what are the conversion rates at each funnel stage," not "how should a system become better, round by round,
at making outreach decisions." This draft uses the PLG funnel as the observation layer for Track 1 (signup, first call,
usage ramp, paid conversion, embedded in workflow), not as the strategy itself.

## 3. What Exactly RSI Improves in GTM

This chapter states what recursive improvement actually improves in GTM, and candidly states which layer current simulation has verified. If RSI in GTM only changed thresholds,
it would not deserve to appear in the title. This section gives the improvement-object table and candidly states which layer current simulation has verified.

### 3.1 Improvement-Object Table: Seven Layers, Each Different

1. **Policy-parameter layer (verified in simulation, see Chapter 7):** outreach thresholds, trust shadow prices,
   waiting thresholds, cooling-off period lengths. This is the narrowest layer, and it was the only layer that the threshold-layer multi-seed experiments had truly run through in simulation before.
   Readers must know: the threshold layer's positive experimental conclusions cover only this layer, and gated recursion is indistinguishable in efficiency from no recursion.
2. **Workflow-topology layer (workflow-and-state joint experiment verified gated vs. ungated/tamperable within synthetic settings, see §7.5; not verified as better than static G):**
   the structure of the outreach sequence itself — light outreach before deep outreach, or partner first then direct sales;
   after three unanswered messages, hand to a human or enter a cooling-off period; how actions across channels are ordered and combined.
   Workflow is not an appendage of policy parameters; it is the skeleton of policy. The workflow-and-state joint experiment's workflow W1
   (light outreach first, forced one-round cooling-off after three unqualified touches) makes gated truth robustly
   beat ungated and verifier-writable variants within synthetic settings, but because no static G arm was set, workflow recursion must not be claimed to beat static strategies on this basis. Layered ablation experiments further tested single-layer effects: the workflow-only gated vs. static paired-difference interval contains zero (see §7.7), so the workflow layer's standalone effect is unconfirmed in the 10-seed ablation; the large-power test (see §7.8) updates this status:
   workflow-only holds in a 1,000-account / 30-seed / 24-week synthetic test (narrow margin, small effect),
   and does not hold in a 1,000-account / 30-seed / 48-week, 4-week-epoch synthetic test (see §7.9),
   so the workflow gain is limited to a one-off small gain under the 24-week design, not shown to scale with time or rounds; the pure-time control and candidate-space expansion tests
   (see §7.10) further qualify that statement: at 48 weeks it remains a marginal effect of the same order, with the interval at the zero line's knife edge,
   and horizon and epoch do not significantly change it; even when the candidate space is expanded to three workflows, recursion depth remains 1; the joint and state layers remain unconfirmed. Strict-superset retesting and independent-validation-seed tests (§7.11) further update this status: under a strict superset, depth is still 1, and richness can be fairly cleanly excluded as the bottleneck; independent validation seeds broke depth 1 in the sense of adoption counts, but total progressive adoptions are 0, and the increment over the same-batch control is unconfirmed, so it must not be written as recursion having been restored. Dual-sample gate and fallback tests (§7.12) update this status once more: under dual-sample confirmation, D's effect almost disappears (E vs. static +0.0010, interval containing zero; E−D0 −0.0079, interval excluding zero), showing that D's adoptions lack cross-sample robustness and mainly come from multiple chances to pass (with the gate-tightening qualification); the fallback arm, under a binary candidate set + independent validation seed design, has almost no increment (F−D0 +0.0000), but that test is unfair and must not be read as the fallback rule being generally invalid; neither dual-sample confirmation nor the fallback rule produced progressive recursion, and total progressive adoptions are 0 in all three arms.
3. **State-representation layer (workflow-and-state joint experiment verified jointly with the workflow layer, see §7.5; standalone effect not separated):**
   how an account's sequential intent is encoded — fundraising, hiring, leadership change, tech-stack change, public technical activity, in-product behavior,
   unified as a decaying event stream rather than a snapshot profile; buyer-committee depth
   (the number of distinct actors in the same account in the same week) as a core feature.
   If the state representation is wrong, even an optimal policy navigates on the wrong map. The workflow-and-state joint experiment's state S1
   (observed intent EMA + trend + fatigue adjustment) was tested jointly with the workflow layer,
   and this draft does not claim to have separated the state layer's own standalone effect. Layered ablation experiments show the state-only vs. static strategy result is a clean zero (see §7.7), so the state layer's standalone effect is unconfirmed.
4. **Scoring-and-routing layer (method design, awaiting verification):** which account is worth pursuing, to what degree,
   assigned to direct sales, partners, or self-serve channels. Scoring is a static snapshot; routing is a sequential policy;
   this draft argues for replacing scoring with policy, not replacing scoring with better scoring.
5. **Evaluator layer (downgraded to a recommendation after the workflow-and-state joint experiment, see §7.5; the most dangerous layer, necessity unproven):**
   how the evaluator itself measures "good" — the main metric definition, harm-metric weights, yield threshold, sensitivity-analysis thresholds.
   Once the evaluator is modified by the very object being improved, it proves itself; therefore evaluator improvement methodologically
   recommends separate versions and separate approval, which is the substance of verification-gate check four, but in the workflow-and-state joint experiment
   the evaluator-separation-approval arm and the gated arm gave completely identical results (truth, claimed value, and harm all differing by 0.0),
   and the approved evaluator tightening changed no policy adoption, so "the evaluator must be separated" is downgraded to a recommendation
   + a zero-harm safe practice, not a necessary condition. This experiment had no control arm in which same-round evaluator improvement caused separation,
   so the necessity of separation is unproven.
6. **Data-and-credit-assignment layer (method design, awaiting verification):** how each round's data is recorded,
   how it is attributed, and how it is used for next-round learning. Behind one closed deal are multiple touchpoints, multiple channels,
   and one offline interaction; if credit assignment is wrong, the next round will reward the wrong behavior.
   This is exactly CollabFlow's trajectory-balancing idea as it corresponds in GTM.
7. **Partner-enablement layer (method design, awaiting verification, see Chapter 8):** how materials, training,
   pricing tools, and co-selling playbooks given to SIs and cloud partners improve with evidence. Partners are not an execution channel for strategy,
   but co-authors of strategy; enablement-package improvements must be consistent with partners' trust budgets.

### 3.2 What RSI in GTM Shares with, and Differs from, General RSI

**Shared:** the improvement objects are all harnesses and workflows, not model weights;
the verification procedure must be independent of the object being verified; verification must be completed before adoption;
veto records are audit assets; cross-task, cross-model generalization must be written into the design, not left as a post-hoc wish.

**GTM-specific differences (three points):**
- **Social feedback:** buyers actively counteract — filtering, blocking, spreading reputation.
  A general task's evaluator is passive; GTM's evaluation environment is alive and adversarial.
- **Irreversible harm:** a failed code task can be rerun; failed trust is hard to recover.
  A blacklisted domain, a delisted partner, a regulatory intervention are not fixed by "trying again next round."
  Therefore GTM's RSI must have an "irreversible actions" list, and those actions always keep a human gate first.
- **Multi-party game:** vendor, cloud partner, SI, and customer contend over the same customer's trust and bill.
  General RSI's "good" is a single score; GTM's "good" is a balance of four parties' budgets.
  An improvement that only improves the vendor's score, while worsening a partner's budget, is a bad improvement.

### 3.3 What RSI in GTM Does Not Improve (Equally Important)

RSI does not improve the model's own weights, does not improve buyers' true willingness to pay,
does not improve partners' revenue-sharing terms themselves (it can only improve "how to negotiate revenue sharing, how to execute revenue sharing,"
not rewrite contracts out of thin air), and does not improve regulatory rules themselves.
Stating these clearly prevents readers from reading RSI as a panacea:
RSI improves how the GTM system learns, how it decides, and how it verifies,
not the world the GTM system faces.

## 4. Problem Formalization: First Principles of Global GTM

### 4.1 Definition (stripped of all job titles, tools, and channel packaging)

> **GTM is matching a model capability to a problem with willingness to pay, with minimal friction and minimal trust loss, and letting that match compound into trust and pipeline for the next match.**

This definition contains no "email," no "sales development representative," no vendor name — only three sets of constraints: friction (time, comprehension cost, procurement cost), trust loss (attention, reputation, relationships), and compounding (whether this deal lowers the cost of the next one). Any GTM action can be judged by these three constraints: Does it lower friction? Does it burn trust? Does it make the next time easier? If any one of the three questions cannot be answered, the action is not worth automating — automation will only make it wrong faster.

### 4.2 Five stages and each stage's North Star (not reply rate)

1. **Discover demand:** Who, on what problem, has reached the point of willingness to pay? The North Star is the number of qualified problems discovered per unit time, not list size.
2. **Prove value:** In the customer's own workflow, has the model measurably improved things? The North Star is measurable improvement in the customer's workflow (time, cost, quality), not how impressive the demo was.
3. **Drive conversion:** What commitment mechanism makes the customer willing to bear switching costs? The North Star is the commitment conversion rate (trial → paid → renewal), not traffic.
4. **Retain and expand:** How does usage climb from a single call to embeddedness in the workflow? The North Star is the retained-usage ramp rate (cohort usage curves), not first-call counts.
5. **Compound trust:** Has this deal lowered friction for the next deal? The North Star is the compounding coefficient (the share of new pipeline coming from existing customers and partners), not total pipeline for the quarter.

The traditional flow diagram (marketing → sales → customer → data → feedback) looks "closed-loop" only because it hides stage 5 inside an arrow. This draft makes stage 5 explicit as the trust budget: compounding is not a metaphor; it is a balance sheet.

### 4.3 Sequential decision formalization: MDP and POMDP

GTM is modelled as a sequential decision process with a trust budget. State includes account time-series intent, in-product behaviour, organisational events, trust already spent, and partnership status; actions include waiting, light outreach, deeply personalised outreach, switching channel, handing off to a human, handing off to an ecosystem partner, raising or lowering outreach intensity, and stopping into a cooling-off period; **waiting is a first-class action**, whose value is not "doing nothing" but "saving the trust budget for the signal window." Reward is discounted qualified pipeline and retained usage, minus trust cost; trust cost splits into an observable part (unsubscribes, complaints, falling deliverability) and an unobservable part (brand damage, estimated with proxy variables and human audit, and stated explicitly in the paper as an estimate, not a measurement). The trust budget is accounted separately across four dimensions — account, channel, brand, and partner — and cannot be transferred across dimensions: budget saved on channel A cannot be spent burning the brand on channel B. The budget's shadow price is priced endogenously: the tighter the budget, the higher the "trust price" of each outreach, and the more restrained the policy.

Causal graph and identification assumptions (summary): signal → intent → outreach → reply → meeting → deal → usage expansion, plus confounding paths (relationships, brand, seasonality, competitor actions, cloud-partner referrals). Stating explicitly where the identification assumptions hold and where they do not is a precondition for the sensitivity analysis in Chapter 6, not a disclaimer in an appendix.

### 4.4 The global compliance regime as a parameter of the formalization, not a footnote

A structural fact of global GTM is that the same action has completely different legality and trust cost under different regimes. This draft writes the regime into the formalization's parameters rather than into the paper's footnotes. The following is a survey-level statement; the official texts govern for specific provisions, and this section cites no single provision verbatim:

- **EU and UK:** Legitimate interest is a common basis, but it requires a documented legitimate-interest assessment and a clear opt-out mechanism; data-subject rights and cross-border transfer rules are hard constraints.
- **United States:** Commercial email centres on opt-out, requiring truthful sender information, clear unsubscribe, and prompt execution; California and other state laws impose separate opt-out and disclosure requirements for the "sale/sharing" of personal information, and the state-law landscape is still expanding, so even within the US market there is no single regime.
- **Canada:** Requirements on commercial electronic messages are the strictest; prior consent is the main line, so provability of data provenance becomes part of GTM itself.
- **China and export rules:** The Personal Information Protection Law imposes strong constraints on data processing and cross-border provision; in recent export-control drafts for comment, the boundary for API, web, app, and SaaS services and technology exports is being drawn, with simplified licensing paths for open-source publication and overseas authorisation, but the specific terms still await official confirmation. This draft does not read "already registered" as "unrestricted going abroad," nor does it read "draft for comment" as "going abroad is banned."

Formalization implication: the compliant action space is a subset of the policy action space — the unreachable set, frequency caps, opt-out mechanisms, and data-residency requirements take different parameter values under different regimes; the same policy form running under different regimes must be instantiated with different parameters. This is exactly why Chapter 9 uses China as a stress-test instance: if the formalization holds only under a single regime, it is not a global formalization.

## 5. Method: Recursive Improvement Under the Verification Gate

### 5.1 From sequential policy to recursive improvement: what exactly is added

A sequential policy answers "should we reach out this round"; recursive self-improvement answers "how does the system get better round by round at making this decision, without improving itself into worse performance." This draft optimises three layers simultaneously:

1. **Policy layer π:** what to do for each account this round.
2. **Harness layer H:** the scaffolding the policy runs on — prompts and workflows, signal processing and state representation, scoring and routing rules, evaluators and log formats. What RSI literature actually improves is this layer, not model weights.
3. **Data layer D:** the new data each round of policy execution produces (which accounts responded under which signals, which outreach burned trust, which waits bought better windows), which is both the next round's training data and the next round's evaluator calibration data.

Recursive form (round k):

```
π_k, H_k ──execute──▶ D_k (including successes, failures, trust consumption, and the counterfactual gap of waiting)
D_k ──offline learning──▶ candidate (π', H')
candidate ──verification gate──▶ pass → (π_{k+1}, H_{k+1}); fail → roll back, record veto reason
Trust budget B accounted throughout; overrun on any dimension → that dimension halts immediately, without waiting for the round to end
```

Key point: **the unit of recursion is a "verified improvement," not "a new round."** Any change that has not passed the verification gate, however clever it looks, does not enter the next round. This is the essential difference between this draft's method and the marketing phrase "self-learning engine." Drawing on the lesson of SpecRSI: candidates may be drafted in parallel, but verification must be completed before adoption; the improver's own learning must keep the true evaluator fixed.

### 5.2 The verification gate: five checks, all required

A candidate improvement must pass all five checks simultaneously, with thresholds written down before the experiment and not changed afterwards:

1. **Simulation hidden-ground-truth test:** On the simulator, pipeline per unit trust beats the incumbent policy, with a confidence-interval lower bound above zero. This guards against overfitting to public metrics while failing on the true mechanism.
2. **Historical-log offline policy evaluation:** Doubly robust or self-normalised inverse-propensity-weighted estimates are likewise positive, and sensitivity analysis for unobserved confounding does not overturn the conclusion. This guards against taking biased logs as truth and replicating the old policy's biases.
3. **Mandatory harm-metric disclosure:** Unsubscribe rate, complaint rate, reputation proxy metrics, and waiting-degradation checks (whether the system has learned to wait forever) must all not worsen. This guards against trading harm for metrics.
4. **Evaluator-independence audit (adoption rule, necessary but not sufficient; independence must be reported in grades):** The evaluator is version-controlled separately from the policy and harness being evaluated; the evaluator itself is not improved in the same round; spot checks verify whether harness changes touch measurement, recording, or retention logic. This guards against harness tampering: changing the score rather than the capability. This draft imposes graded requirements on "independence" and must not write a lower grade as a higher one: (a) version separation; (b) write-permission separation; (c) frozen evaluation / original-image replay (evaluation replayed on the original image, not scored inside a container the agent can modify); (d) veto logs as first-class citizens; (e) controlled reporting of prompt and context engineering (prompts themselves can move cheating rates substantially, so prompts, context, and scaffolding must be reported together with results, never reporting only the model and the evaluator).
   **Limits of read-only testing (must be stated):** Read-only access blocks test-modification-type false passes, but cannot eliminate other cheating such as special-casing and operator overloading; hidden tests come close to eliminating false passes but at the cost of legitimate performance loss; monitors' detection rates vary greatly across task styles (per the detection-rate interval cited from a publicly reported benchmark, roughly 86–89% for LiveCodeBench-style tasks and only roughly 42–65% for SWE-bench-style tasks; this interval has not been checked against the original report). Therefore, even with all five items in place, the adoption rule may only be written as "substantially reduces the risk of false improvements," never as "guarantees no false improvements." Evaluator separation itself was downgraded to a recommendation after the workflow-and-state joint experiment (see §3.1); it is not a necessary condition.
5. **Trust budget audit:** Budget consumption on all four dimensions is within limits; overrun on any dimension is a veto, and surpluses on other dimensions may not offset it. This guards against the tragedy of the commons: one team's win, the whole company's bill.

**Rollback and veto logs are first-class citizens:** Every vetoed candidate in every round, the veto reason, and the version rolled back to must enter the paper's appendix and the benchmark's audit trail. "Recursive improvement" with no veto record is scientifically untrustworthy.

### 5.3 Trust shadow price and threshold interaction

The trust budget is priced endogenously as a Lagrange multiplier: every outreach has a "trust shadow price," and the tighter the budget, the more restrained the policy. Threshold and shadow-price interaction must be reported together: reporting thresholds without shadow prices leaves the reader unable to tell whether the policy's restraint comes from the budget or from chance; reporting shadow prices without thresholds leaves the reader unable to reproduce the policy. The current simulation validates only the threshold-and-shadow-price layer (see §3.1); improvements to workflows, state representation, scoring and routing, evaluators, and data credit assignment are method design and future work in this draft, not validated conclusions.

### 5.4 Where personalised generation sits: the execution head, not the policy itself

The generation module is under two hard constraints: first, verifiability — every personalised assertion must have a traceable source, and without a source it is not written; second, trust audit — generated content passes an "AI-slop detector" before sending (adversarially exercised against buyer filters, but never with bypassing buyer filters as the optimisation objective). Policy decides "whether to send, and at what level"; generation decides only "how to write it." Reversing this order — generating first and then deciding whether to send — is the most common structural error in Agentic GTM.

### 5.5 The five-layer end-state system and the automation order

The end state is not "a smarter sales-development-representative robot" but a five-layer system: perception layer, decision layer (the core of this draft), execution layer, learning layer, and audit layer, with human gating retained only on high-risk actions — large customers, brand, compliance, partner revenue-sharing — and the gating itself gradually relaxed as evidence accumulates. **Principle of automation order:** automate perception and execution first (low cost, reversible), then low-risk decisions (small customers, light outreach), and only last high-risk decisions. Reversing the order — letting the system automatically send deep outreach to large customers first — is the most common way GTM automation dies. "Humans entirely unnecessary" is an end state, not a starting point.

## 6. GTM-PolicyBench: Simulator, Offline Evaluation Layer, and Trial Protocol

> Positioning: this chapter is a benchmark design plus one simulator implementation; it has not yet become a public benchmark (no public release, third-party tasks, or external baseline implementations); positive conclusions are all confined to the synthetic setting, see Chapter 11.

### 6.1 The simulator: why a simulator that can "burn trust" is needed

The simulator generates synthetic account trajectories: organisational events, intent evolution (a latent-variable Markov process), in-product usage, outreach responses, and trust erosion. The key mechanism is the negative-feedback loop of trust dynamics: high-frequency, low-quality outreach causes reputation decay, which in turn causes subsequent deliverability to fall — a link generally missing from existing benchmarks. If trust cannot be burned in the simulator, then "learning when not to sell" can never be learned, and the benchmark itself is invalid. The task is: given the observed history, output an outreach policy for the next 90 days; evaluation is on long-term pipeline and trust consumption under hidden ground truth. The primary metric is qualified pipeline per unit trust consumed (Pipeline-per-Trust); reply rate is a diagnostic metric only and does not count toward ranking; complaints, unsubscribes, and reputation changes are mandatorily disclosed.

### 6.2 Primary metric, output threshold, and Pareto frontier

The default output threshold is 200: policies with absolute pipeline below the threshold do not enter the primary-metric ranking, while the (absolute pipeline, primary metric) Pareto frontier is reported alongside. Threshold sensitivity must be reported in the same table: raising the threshold to 500 favours high-output policies, while too low a threshold inflates "barely-doing-anything" ratio-gaming policies. **There is no single champion**: on seed 42's Pareto frontier, reply-rate-greedy, human rules, and gated recursion coexist, each occupying a different position on output versus efficiency. The paper must report both output and efficiency dimensions; a ranking reporting only the ratio or only the total is invalid.

### 6.3 Trajectory-level fitted-Q evaluation: a ranking heuristic, not a measurement

The evaluation unit is the whole trajectory, not each week: pipeline is counted only in the first qualifying week, and is constantly zero thereafter; trust continues to accumulate after qualification, because the policy may still reach out after qualification. The primary metric is a ratio of expectations, E[pipeline]/E[trust], not a weekly reward average and not E[pipeline/trust]. All states uniformly take pre-action values, to rule out leakage. Conclusion (synthetic setting, see Chapter 7): fitted-Q ranking is broadly recoverable (main-experiment Spearman 0.9), but magnitude bias changes sign with seed and policy, so it **may only be used as a ranking heuristic, not as a measurement**. Human rules are explicitly excluded from the offline-evaluation ranking because their internal counter is the target policy's own counterfactual history, which cannot be recovered from logging trajectories; they are reported only in ground truth — this is an identifiability boundary, not an omission.

### 6.4 Why naive bandit offline evaluation is unusable

Naive bandit methods compute rewards repeatedly by week, but qualification happens only once, so they overestimate weekly mean reward by roughly 1.7–2.1× [synthetic support]. This is a direct consequence of absorbing-state carryover, not a sample-size problem: larger samples will not repair repeated counting; they will only make the wrong estimate more precise. Any bandit offline-evaluation table that reports only ranking, without stating how absorbing states were handled, is invalid.

### 6.5 Why sequential doubly robust evaluation is unusable under a 24-step deterministic policy

Cumulative importance weights in sequential doubly robust evaluation decay exponentially with horizon. Under a 24-step deterministic policy, the effective sample size in the main experiment decays from about 18 in week 7 to about 5 in week 15 and 0 in week 23; bootstrap standard errors can reach about 7× the estimate. The sequential doubly robust ranking correlation of 1.0 in the main experiment is a coincidence and **must not be cited**. Shortening the horizon to 8 weeks does not solve the fundamental problem (effective sample size is already only about 18 in week 7, and delayed pipeline would be discarded). The feasible validation layers are trajectory-level fitted Q (ranking heuristic) + simulation counterfactuals + future shadow mode and cluster randomised trials. Randomising the target policy and richer fitted-Q state representations are candidate next methods, not implemented and not claimed at this stage.

### 6.6 Sensitivity analysis: seed-level bounds and the Warm confounding grid — their meanings must not be confused

**Seed-level Wilcoxon Gamma bounds:** Treat the 10 seeds' paired differences as paired observations; this is seed-level sensitivity, **not** a real-world causal bound. Gated beats ungated with Gamma bounds of 3.0 at both 1,000 accounts (10/10 wins) and 200 accounts (9/10 wins); gated beats no-recursion becomes non-significant already at Gamma equal to 1 under both sample sizes.

**Warm unobserved-confounding grid:** In the synthetic setting, when the share of warm accounts' logging actions rewritten as deep outreach rises from 0.5 to 0.9, the fitted-Q conclusion of gated beating ungated flips; under strong confounding combinations, fitted-Q ranking collapses entirely (Spearman 0.0), even when the true difference is largest. Fitted-Q magnitudes are also systematically overestimated across the Warm grid. That is: unobserved confounding need not be infinitely strong — rewriting 90% of warm accounts' logging actions suffices for fitted Q to say the opposite; sales intuition, relationships, and offline information in real GTM logs are sources of exactly this magnitude, so any historical-log fitted-Q conclusion must be accompanied by sensitivity analysis. The Warm grid's flip threshold is a number under the synthetic setting, not a measurement on real logs.

### 6.7 Prospective trial protocol: shadow mode and cluster randomised trials

Shadow mode for 4–8 weeks (the policy advises, humans decide, disagreements recorded) → cluster randomised trials (randomised by account, geography, and channel clusters, to avoid contamination within the same account) → go-live decision gate (release at scale only when pipeline improvement at equal trust and non-worsening trust consumption both hold). The China version and the outbound version of the protocol share the same formalization with different action spaces and trust-dynamics parameters, but at this stage only the protocol is delivered, with no promise that any vendor will accept it.

### 6.8 Public-signal prototype: proxy prediction, not conversion-rate prediction

The public-signal prototype predicts "ramp," not "registration": whether a model is still on public integration and hosting lists after 8 weeks, not how many registrations it obtained. The three subtasks are adoption-heat nowcasting, ramp-proxy prediction, and decay warning, all at segment/cohort level, not individual level. Metrics are directional accuracy, rank correlation, calibration, lead time, and false-alarm rate; **no "conversion-rate improvement percentage" is reported, because there is no conversion rate in public signals.** Failure modes are written down in advance: token inflation, launch pulses, hosting-provider structural change, price-war confounding, survivor bias. Real collection at this stage is a negative result (see Chapter 7); the next step should be an event timeline first.

## 7. Experiments: Main Results Table and Phased Reporting (all synthetic data)

All results in this chapter are synthetic data from the in-house simulator GTM-Sim and its later versions, not any real vendor's data. The primary metric is qualified pipeline per unit trust consumed (Pipeline-per-Trust); reply rate is a diagnostic metric only and does not count toward ranking; complaints, unsubscribes, and reputation changes are mandatorily disclosed. The criterion for "beats" is uniform: the 95% t-interval of the paired difference must exclude zero; an interval containing zero is judged "not established," and the verdict is not changed by the sign of the mean or by a win-count majority. Empty-comparison seeds (seeds where the verification gate adopted nothing in any epoch, with parameters identical to the initial configuration) are all retained in the paired calculations and must not be removed; empty-comparison seeds pull paired differences toward zero and dilute wins into ties, rather than inflating the advantage. Full phased numbers are in Appendix D; the main text of this chapter retains only core results and verdicts.

### 7.4 Phase 4: Real Collection of Public Signals (Negative Result)

### 7.5 Phase 5: Joint Workflow-and-State Experiment — Four Arms (Synthetic Data)

Thresholds were preregistered before the experiment, and any failure triggers the corresponding downgrade; this section reports only the post-preregistration results and does not relax thresholds after the fact. Report files: phase2a/phase2d_report.md and phase2d_results.json. Workflow W1: light-outreach-first (after three outreach attempts without qualification, forced stop and one cool-down round); state S1: snapshot observed intent replaced by an event-stream representation (observed-intent EMA + trend + fatigue adjustment). Each epoch (8 weeks) generates 4 candidates, each of which changes at least one layer of the workflow or the state representation; validation set 200 accounts / 8 weeks / independent seeds, with validation-set ground truth computed by the frozen evaluator. The four arms share the same simulator, the same signal distribution, the same budget, and paired seeds, with 1,000 accounts, 10 seeds, 24 weeks: B gated, evaluator frozen; C same object, ungated, adoption by internal proxy score; D same object, verifier writable — internal reports rewritten as 20% ground truth + 80% proxy; E same as B, except evaluator improvement goes through an independent approval cycle. False adoption is strictly defined as: a candidate adopted in some epoch whose internal score is higher than the incumbent's internal score, but whose validation-set ground-truth pipeline/trust is not higher than the incumbent's ground truth.

Results are in Tables 1a and 1b; E's main-run results are exactly identical to B's (E's 25 vetoes = 13 policy-gate vetoes + 12 evaluator-improvement vetoes; evaluator improvements had a further 8 approvals, but the approved tightenings changed no subsequent policy-adoption outcome), so evaluator separation is downgraded to a recommendation, not a necessary condition.

**Three limitations that must be reported together.**

1. **No static-policy arm, so no claim of beating the static policy may be made.** This experiment set no static-policy arm, so "gated workflow + state recursion beats the static policy" was not tested in this experiment; the threshold layer's "gated ≈ no-recursion" still stands; this experiment's 10/10 win–loss is a comparison of B against C/D, not of B against the static policy. This limitation was followed up by the layered ablation experiment in §7.7, whose conclusion is unproven (see §7.7).
2. **Empty-comparison seeds stated explicitly, not dropped.** Under seeds 0, 3, and 4, B/E adopted nothing in either epoch; this report retains all 10 seeds and does not drop them; dropping the empty-comparison seeds would make the B − C/D difference larger, but this draft does not draw conclusions from post-dropping numbers.

### 7.7 Phase 7: Layered Ablation Experiment — Static Control and Single-Layer Ablations (Synthetic Data)

This section is the core experiment; report files phase2a/phase2f_report.md and phase2f_results.json. Statistical discipline follows the same standard as the joint experiment: 1,000 accounts / 10 seeds / 24 weeks, paired comparisons on the same batch of seeds, reporting means, 95% t-intervals, and win–loss counts, with empty-comparison seeds never dropped.

**Method (same calibre as the joint experiment).** Same simulator, same signal distribution, same budget, same main metric, same false-adoption definition, same harm metric; validation set 200 accounts / 8 weeks / independent seeds. Static-policy arm: threshold-layer trust-constrained policy, no recursion, fixed configuration throughout {workflow baseline, state baseline, threshold 0.40, trust shadow price 3.0}, the same calibre as the no-recursion policy in the threshold-layer multi-seed experiment, and also the epoch-0 starting configuration of each arm in the joint experiment. **Results.** Arm means and paired differences are in Tables 1a and 1b; per-seed ground truth and full numbers are in Appendix D. By the preregistered verdict: **recursion gain unproven and contracted.** Joint gated minus static policy +0.0161, interval [−0.009, +0.041] containing zero, win/loss/tie 5/2/3, triggering the preregistered contraction clause: **the conclusion contracts to "the gate vetoes bad recursion; recursion gain is unproven even synthetically."** Workflow-only and state-only also both failed to meet the "beats the static policy" standard, so there is no case of "only one layer confirmed," still less an interaction case of "both layers individually ineffective but jointly effective" — the interaction-effect hypothesis is not triggered and must not be proposed.

**Interpretation (four points that must be reported together).**

1. **Joint recursion did not beat the static policy, and this is not a "near miss" rhetoric problem.** The joint-minus-static interval contains zero and includes 2 negative seeds (seed 7 at −0.0305, seed 9 at −0.0333): adoptions that pass the gate on the validation set can be negative on the main run — the gate guarantees validation-set improvement, not main-run improvement. Validation set and main run differ in account population, duration, and the subsequent evolution of adopted configurations, and transfer of validation gain to the main run is itself an unproven link.
2. **Workflow-only is directionally consistent but below standard, and must not be reported as "only one layer confirmed."** All 4 non-tie differences for workflow-only minus static policy are positive, none negative, mean +0.0131, but the 95% interval lower bound is −0.001 (barely containing zero). By the preregistered interval standard, it remains "unproven." The honest statement can only be: the workflow layer is the most promising next candidate among the three arms (4/4 non-ties positive), but it did not cross the line at this experiment's scale and needs an independent test at larger sample or longer horizon, rather than being upgraded in this draft.
3. **State-only is a clean zero.** State-only minus static policy mean −0.0007, interval symmetric and containing zero, non-ties 2 wins 2 losses; of state-only's 4 adoptions, 2 are negative on the main run, and the joint-minus-state-only positive mean (+0.0168) likewise has an interval containing zero. The state representation (event-stream EMA + trend + fatigue adjustment) has no attributable standalone effect in this synthetic setting; the joint arm's positive mean cannot be split-attributed to the state layer.
4. **Joint does not beat either single layer; the interaction hypothesis is not triggered.** Joint minus workflow-only +0.0030 (interval containing zero, 2 wins 4 losses), joint minus state-only +0.0168 (interval containing zero) — the joint arm does not even beat workflow-only on win–loss counts. The preregistered interaction clause requires "both layers individually ineffective but jointly effective"; this experiment is "jointly ineffective too," so the interaction-effect hypothesis has neither a trigger condition nor licence to be proposed as a post hoc explanation.

**Relation to the threshold-layer and joint-layer conclusions.** The threshold-layer multi-seed experiment's "gated ≈ no-recursion" is not overturned but extended: the layered ablation experiment reaches the same-shaped conclusion at the workflow and state layers (joint ≈ static policy, interval containing zero); the joint experiment's "gated beats ungated/tampered" is reproduced per seed in this batch's rerun and stands. The layered ablation experiment set no absorbing-state arm and no evaluator-separation arm, so it does not change the trust-budget, adoption-rule, or evaluator-separation verdicts, nor strengthen them. Sensitivity disclosure on never dropping empty-comparison seeds: if empty-comparison seeds were dropped, the joint-minus-static non-empty-seed mean would rise to +0.0230 (5 wins / 2 losses, n=7), and the workflow-only-minus-static non-empty-seed mean would rise to +0.0328 (4 wins / 0 losses, n=4); this draft does not draw conclusions from post-dropping numbers, because conditioning on the post hoc variable "whether the gate adopted" is selection bias, and dropped numbers are sensitivity disclosure only and must not enter the main conclusion.

## 8. Two Canonical Tracks: Where the Method Must Be Written Concretely

This chapter writes the tracks as action spaces that must be executable and auditable in the method and experimental design. Chapter 8 retains the action-space tables; the two tracks share Chapter 4's formalization, trust budget, and verification gate, and differ in action space, trust-dynamics parameters, and compliance-regime parameters. The current simulation only verifies that the formalization can run under a synthetic dual track; cross-track transfer (tooling-stack improvements partially transferring between tracks) remains unfinished and must not be claimed as verified generalization.

### 8.1 Track 1: Developer API GTM — The Track That Can Run First

Why is developer API the first track? Because its behaviour is observable, measurable, and automatable, and it does not first require solving the hardest problem — "who is the decision-maker": the developer is simultaneously the user, the evaluator, and the payer (or the payer's direct driver). The chain's north star is the ramp after the first call (cohort usage curves), not the first call itself; developer-community reputation is this track's most fragile trust asset, and the cost of one over-marketing episode is not one unsubscribe but a community-wide label of "this vendor only sends ads."

| Chain stage | Action space (auditable) | Observable signals / metrics | Trust budget accounting point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery | Documentation and example pushes (light outreach) | Documentation visits, example runs, repository references | Account × channel budget |
| Sandbox and first call | Free quota, sandbox environment, one-click runnable examples | First-call count (diagnostic metric, not the north star) | Light-outreach frequency |

### 8.2 Track 2: Partner / Ecosystem GTM — The Multi-Party Trust Track

The partner track is not a "supplement to direct sales"; for open-weights vendors it is often the main battleground: when models reach customers through cloud marketplaces, SIs, and industry partners, the vendor does not directly own the customer relationship. This track's trust budget is a four-party trust budget: vendor, cloud partner, SI, and customer. An improvement that only makes the vendor's score better, while making the partner budget worse, is a bad improvement, and the verification gate must veto it on the partner dimension.

## 10. Limitations and Ethics

### 10.1 Methodological Limitations

- Trust proxy metrics not actually measured: reputation, unsubscribe, and complaint in the simulation are set mechanisms, not actually measured brand damage; the entire trust budget formalization rests on a construct not yet actually measured.
- Simulation–reality gap: the simulator's trust dynamics are modelling assumptions, not measured physical laws; if real buyer filtering behaviour differs greatly from the simulation, the simulation conclusions must be discounted.
- Evaluation applicability boundaries: fitted-Q ranking depends entirely on whether state keys are sufficient, and magnitudes are not credible; sequential doubly robust evaluation fails under deterministic policies and long horizons; randomized target policies and richer fitted-Q state representations are not yet implemented.
- Verification-gate boundary conditions: when signal windows are sparse (organization-event probability 0.04), verification-gate efficiency turns negative; under some seeds the verification-gate parameters never changed at all, in which case the comparison is uninformative.
- Generalization boundaries: API scenarios and large-enterprise-deal scenarios differ enormously in reward delay and decision-chain length; if cross-scenario tests fail, conclusions should shrink to scenario-specific methods, not universal theorems.

### 10.2 Data and Evidence Limitations

- No interviews, no real logs, no partnership data: all of this draft's positive conclusions are confined to synthetic settings.
- Public-signal limitations: real weekly-panel collection is a negative result, and the predictive value of public signals for ramp rate is unproven; state representations must rely on in-product behavioural data, not only on public signals.
- Source tiering: vendor self-reports, media accounts, financial-report second-hand accounts, and public signals must not be mixed; sources not checked against the original text are stated throughout as downgraded wording matching their evidence tier (media accounts, second-hand accounts, or undisclosed).

### 10.3 Ethics and Compliance Red Lines

- Do not use "optimization" as a cover for circumventing buyer filters: what is optimized is "outreach worth receiving," not "techniques for bypassing filters."
- Do not crawl, buy, or infer personal private data; do not directly claim public-signal heat as conversion rate.
- Do not treat media-reported revenue targets, valuations, and listing plans as financial-statement facts; do not treat public-platform token volume as revenue; do not claim any conversion-rate improvement figure before partnership data exist.
- Do not directly launch a "Chinese Clay"; do not take cold-email reply rate as the north star; do not take GTM headcount as a measure of demand scale.
- The compliance action space must be written into the formalization, not into a footnote; unreachable sets, frequency caps, opt-out mechanisms, and data-residency requirements have different parameters under different regimes.

### 10.4 Ethics Extended: Buyer Autonomy, Filter-Adversarial Boundaries, Spam Boundaries, and the Ethical Limits of the Verification Gate

This section is a dedicated ethics section, not a repetition of the compliance checklist; it answers a question §10.3 does not answer head-on: even if the verification gate vetoes bad recursion within the simulation, and even if the trust budget is accounted for, in what sense might a GTM system still be illegitimate? The answer has four parts, each of which takes precedence over any efficiency claim.

**1. Buyer autonomy takes precedence over seller efficiency.** The trust budget measures the depletion of a seller asset, not the degree of buyer autonomy. A system can "efficiently" get a buyer to accept outreach in a state of ignorance, unwillingness, or inability to exit, all within its trust budget — the budget is not overspent, yet autonomy has already been violated. This draft's formalization must therefore coexist with an independent autonomy constraint: verifiable consent (auditable opt-in), enforceable exit (unsubscribe taking effect in a single action and not delayed by a "retention flow"), and understandable identity disclosure (who is reaching out, why they are contacting, and where the data came from — none of the three may be omitted). Any writing that argues outreach is legitimate because "the trust budget was not overspent" is an impermissible upgrade; the budget is internal seller discipline, not a substitute for buyer consent.

**2. Filter-adversarial boundary: optimize "outreach worth receiving," not "techniques for bypassing filters."** Buyer filters (spam filters, domain-reputation systems, platform review, buyer organizations' blocking rules) are enforcement mechanisms of buyer autonomy, not obstacles to be "optimized away." This draft draws a hard boundary: improvement candidates whose purpose is evasion, deception, disguise, split identity, or borrowing another party's reputation must not enter the verification gate even if they raise reply rate or pipeline within the simulation — they are excluded at the candidate-generation layer, not left for the verification gate to veto. None of the verification gate's five checks is a check on "whether a filter was bypassed," so the gate cannot endorse filter adversarialism; any statement writing "passing the verification gate" as "filter adversarialism is acceptable" is an error. Counterexample test: if a candidate's sole source of improvement is making it harder for a filter to identify it, that candidate should be excluded no matter how high its score.

**3. Spam boundary: volume itself is harm.** The trust budget uses "pipeline per unit of trust" as its efficiency yardstick, but spam harm does not occur only when trust is exhausted — high-frequency, low-relevance, easily batch-replicable outreach is itself an occupation of the buyer's attention commons, even if a single outreach triggers no unsubscribe or complaint. Harm disclosure in this draft must therefore include two dimensions, frequency and relevance (outreach frequency distribution, relevance of outreach to account intent), and cannot report only the three ex post metrics of unsubscribe rate, complaint rate, and reputation change; a system whose frequency doubles while its unsubscribe rate is unchanged must not be reported as "harm unchanged." The output threshold (Chapter 6) and waiting as a first-class action (Chapter 4) are this draft's partial responses to this, but not sufficient responses: frequency caps in real deployment must be jointly determined by the buyer regime and platform rules, and must not be reverse-engineered from seller efficiency targets.

**4. The ethical limits of the verification gate: the gate cannot substitute for buyer consent and legal compliance.** The verification gate is the seller's internal recursion-safety mechanism; its objects of inspection are candidate improvements (whether they beat the incumbent on the validation set, whether they are independent of the evaluator, whether harms are disclosed), not the legality or legitimacy of the outreach act itself. Gate passage does not equal outreach being lawful, and gate veto does not equal outreach being unlawful; the gate has no say on "whether this person should be contacted," and no adjudicative power over consent, disclosure, and data-residency requirements under regimes such as GDPR / CAN-SPAM / CASL / CCPA. No "gated" statement anywhere in this draft may therefore be paraphrased as "compliant" or "consent obtained"; before deployment there must be separate independent compliance review and buyer-consent checks, and the verification gate is only one layer of internal discipline within them, not the last line of defence, still less the only one. Writing the verification gate as a compliance guarantee is expressly prohibited in this draft.

## 11. Conclusions (Full Version: The Only Complete Conclusion Statement in This Draft)

The next generation of GTM is not "an agent that writes emails better," but "a GTM system that improves itself" — each round of improvement must first prove it has not burned trust before it earns entry to the next round. In this draft, that sentence is a problem vision and a future-work direction, not a proven mechanism conclusion: the effectiveness of the recursive-improvement architecture itself, its gain over a static policy, and its generality are all unproven by this draft's evidence. ## 12. Future Work

1. **Randomized target policies and richer fitted-Q state representations:** the precondition is first proving, in simulation, that ranking and magnitudes recover simultaneously; otherwise they must not enter real logs.
2. **Event-timeline-first public panels:** if weekly panels are infeasible, the predictive value of public signals contracts to "unproven."
3. **Shadow-mode partnership research:** de-identified log offline evaluation, shadow mode, cluster randomized trials, real measurement of partner revenue shares and net retention, and cross-vendor generalization tests.
4. **Complete validation of tooling-stack improvements:** workflow topology, state representation, scoring routing, evaluator, and data-credit allocation improvements are currently only method designs, and need verifiable experiments designed layer by layer, not only threshold-layer validation. The joint experiment has jointly validated workflow + state layers within the simulation (gated vs. ungated/tampered), and the stratified ablation experiment has added static controls and single-layer ablations: the workflow layer holds within the simulation in the large-power test (marginal, small effect, see §7.8), the scalability test (§7.9) has executed the "longer horizon + denser epochs" prescription and judged it fragile and non-scaling, the pure-time control and candidate-space expansion test (§7.10) has executed the "48-week + 8-week epoch" control arm and change-candidate-generation prescription: the pure-time effect is small (arm A +0.0059 close to the large-power test's +0.0075, with the 24-week checkpoint exactly equal value by value), epoch attribution has been corrected (arm A and the scalability test differ by only +0.0003, a knife-edge crossing rather than a substantive epoch effect), depth 1 still recurs in the three-workflow space, but arm B's candidate set is not a strict superset of arm A's, and the strict-superset retest and independent-validation-seed test have both been executed (see §7.11): the former still has depth 1 and the added variants changed no endpoint result, so richness has been fairly cleanly excluded; the latter broke depth 1 in the adoption-count sense, but total progressive adoptions are 0, and the increment vs. the same-batch control is unproven. The dual-sample gate and fallback test has also been executed (see §7.12): under dual-sample confirmation, D's effect almost disappears (E vs. static +0.0010, interval including zero; E−D0 −0.0079, interval excluding zero), indicating that D's adoptions lack cross-sample robustness and come mainly from multiple chances to pass (with the gate-strictness qualification); the fallback arm, under the binary candidate set + independent-validation-seed design, has almost no increment (F−D0 +0.0000, only 1 fallback adoption in 150 evaluation slots), but that test is unfair and must not be read as the fallback rule being generally ineffective; neither dual-sample confirmation nor the fallback rule produced progressive recursion, with total progressive adoptions 0 in all three arms. The next-step priority order (changing only one thing at a time, all preregistered) is therefore:
   (a) A fair fallback test — a "fallback × strict superset (8 candidates) × independent validation seeds" arm, differing from D0 only in the gate rule (when the best candidate fails, fall back to the suboptimal candidate that passes), with the same candidate set as §7.11 arm C; seeds 6 and 29 are valid clean test cases in that arm (if W2, the best candidate, fails and W1, the suboptimal candidate, passes, it should convert to adoption).
   (b) Separating E's strictness confound — a larger-confirmation-sample arm, or a two-sample pooled-estimation arm (pooling the two samples and then judging once with the same gate rule, rather than two independent passes), differing from D0 only in the confirmation method; if that arm's adoption rate returns to near D0's, then E's collapse comes mainly from the stricter gate rather than luck; if that arm still returns to depth 1, then the multiple-chances-to-pass explanation is strengthened.
   (c) Before (a)/(b) are done, no more same-specification tests with longer horizons, more seeds, or more workflow layers may be run — they are expected only to repeat the already-known patterns of "first-round adoption, then all vetoes" (reused validation), "adoptions without progression" (independent validation), or "adoptions sharply reduced after confirmation" (dual-sample gate); the state layer is a clean zero and is no longer prioritized for same-specification repeat tests.
5. **Controlled adoption-rule comparison (later, not a blocker for this draft):** on a single benchmark (ImpossibleBench-style), manipulate only verifier access (full read/write vs. read-only vs. hidden), fix model, prompt, and scaffolding, and report false-improvement rates (impossible-task pass rates) and legitimate-task performance under each condition, with confidence intervals and per-model tables; start directly from that paper's tables/code for replication or citation, and do not again substitute second-hand directional description for rate tables.
6. **Rewrite the public-test preregistration (later, not a blocker for this draft):** abandon the original "public blocklist event" definition, and adopt a retrievable operational definition (e.g., a public spam-label breakpoint, or a repository opt-in / interaction-restriction breakpoint), and confirm a BigQuery project or GitHub Search API channel before execution; if the channel is still unavailable, the public test should be explicitly recorded as "not done, open," and must not again be passed off with system-level material as account-level testing. This draft does not write these two future-work items as completed, nor use them to back this draft's conclusions.
7. **Things no longer prioritized:** continuing to expand simulation seed counts to "farm" the win/loss count for gated over non-recursive (the difference has already narrowed to near zero); using doubly robust ranking correlation 1.0 as evidence; making conditional claims from a 3-seed mechanism scan; upgrading synthetic wording into real conclusions.

### 9.4 Four Shared Needs That Are Insufficiently Recognised (Hypotheses, To Be Verified)

1. **Customer-acquisition-cost discipline under thin margins:** not the cost of acquiring a user, but the cost per retained token; financial reports disclose only revenue and gross margin, not channel-level acquisition cost, while team KPIs remain registrations and usage.
2. **Partner shelf power:** who owns the customer relationship, pricing power, and data boundary; each cloud partnership is negotiated separately, there is no unified cross-partner view, and "listed" is treated as "sold".
3. **Developer ramp rate:** conversion from first call to sustained calls, and then to embedding in a workflow; the first call is cheap and easy to obtain, so teams easily mistake the first call for success.
4. **Overseas enterprise trust:** compliance, data residency, attribution, revenue share, and dispute incidents; treated as a legal or public-relations afterthought, rather than an upfront GTM constraint.

## 13. Submission-Path Suggestions (Not Final)

This section is not a submission decision, does not rearrange the full text to any venue's format, and does not set any venue as a decided matter. It only explains, on the basis of this draft's current evidence form, the risks and reasons for each of three paths, for reference at a later decision; the final decision on venue and language will be made only after the content stabilises (the test after the candidate-generation change, the remaining citations' full-text checks, and the formal drafting of the mechanism figure and main-result figure), and no path may be read outside this section as already decided.

**1. Risk of submitting directly to a main-conference main track (NeurIPS / ICML-type).**
There are three hard gaps between this draft's evidence form and the main track's conventional expectations: (a) the central conclusion's empirical evidence all comes from a self-built simulator, trust dynamics are encoded assumptions rather than measurements, and reviewers can reasonably question that "the simulation proves only the simulator" (§10.1); (b) the recursion gain itself is unproven — the joint and state layers are zero or include zero, the workflow layer holds only marginally and narrowly in the 24-week design and does not hold in the 48-week design (§7.8, §7.9), while the verification gate's veto value, though robust, is not equivalent to a recursion-improvement efficiency gain; the independent-validation-seed test (§7.11) also did not change this judgement: D's increment interval against the same-batch control includes zero; (c) GTM-PolicyBench in this draft remains a benchmark design + one simulator implementation, with no public version, third-party tasks, or external baseline implementations, and the citation status remains a graded result (10 items with key sections checked, the rest second-hand citations, media-reported, or undisclosed). Before these gaps are filled, submitting directly to the main track carries a high probability of rejection, and the rejection reasons will most likely concentrate on the above three points rather than writing details; submitting early will not accelerate impact, it will only consume a review round.

**2. Reasons for an arXiv preprint + benchmark code release first.**
This is this draft's most honest global-impact path at present, for three reasons: (a) a preprint allows this draft to be fully read and cited in its contracted form — "formalisation + benchmark design + veto evidence + negative results" — without packaging synthetic conclusions as a verified general mechanism; (b) benchmark code release (simulator, trajectory-level offline evaluation, clustered RCT protocol, preregistration and veto-log formats) is the only path to turn a "design" into an "adoptable benchmark" — if this draft has any impact, it can only come via benchmark adoption, and adoption starts from runnable, reproducible code, not from a cover letter; (c) after the preprint + code release, external researchers can test the trust budget and the adoption rule on real logs or their own simulations, which is exactly the evidence type this draft most lacks, and it does not require this draft first to claim it already has it. Preconditions: before release, the preprint note must truthfully label the graded evidence status and the synthetic-only limitation, and must not weaken them; unchecked items have already been stated as downgraded wording and must not be written as checked.

**3. Reasons for specialised conferences / workshops as a second path.**
Compared with the main track, specialised conferences and workshops (for example, specialised venues in agent safety and evaluation, AI for science / business agents, and offline evaluation and causal inference) are more tolerant of "method design + negative results + benchmark proposal" manuscripts, and reviewers are more likely to give substantive feedback on the verification-gate design, the evaluation-method falsifications, and the candidate-generation bottleneck itself, rather than rejecting only for "no real data". This draft's negative-results part (naive bandit overestimation, sequential doubly robust failure, FQE only ranking, the public-panel collection negative result, the GitHub main test not executed, and the 48-week non-scaling) is a contribution here, not a defect; but even on the second path, synthetic conclusions must not be upgraded to real conclusions, and the specific venue choice still awaits content stabilisation — this section does not name any specific conference as a decided target.

**This section's bottom line (consistent with the full text):** Whichever path is taken, this draft's conclusion ceiling is unchanged — real-vendor effects, conversion-rate improvements, cross-vendor generalisation, the trust budget as a general primitive, the adoption rule as a general principle, and evaluator separation as a necessary condition all still cannot be claimed; the workflow gain can still only be stated as "a one-off small gain in the 24-week design, still a same-order marginal effect at 48 weeks, with the interval at the zero-line knife-edge, schedule and epoch not significantly changing it, and not proven to scale with time or rounds", and it must not be turned back into "workflow recursion gain proven" because arm A's lower bound is barely positive. Independent validation seeds are only to be stated as breaking depth 1 in the adoption-count sense, must not be written as recursion restored or strengthening with rounds, and D−STATIC significance must not be substituted for the D−A0 comparison (§7.11). Under dual-sample confirmation the effect almost disappears (E vs. static +0.0010, interval including zero; E−D0 −0.0079, interval excluding zero), showing that D's adoptions lack cross-sample robustness and mainly come from multiple pass opportunities (with the gate-stricter qualifier); the fallback arm under the binary candidate set + independent validation seed design has almost no increment (F−D0 +0.0000), but that test is unfair and must not be read as the fallback rule being generally invalid; neither dual-sample confirmation nor the fallback rule produced progressive recursion, and progressive adoption totals are 0 in all three arms (§7.12).
